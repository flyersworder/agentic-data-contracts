Context layers, curated documentation that an analytics agent fetches at query time, produce large accuracy gains on text-to-SQL benchmarks, but a with/without comparison cannot say which part of the layer does the work. Using a data contract as the instrument, we separate the knowledge a layer carries from the scaffolding and format that deliver it, on DABStep and LiveSQLBench, across seven models, with 53 predictions fixed before their runs. The knowledge does the work: emptying the contract's prose while keeping its tools and rules costs 26 to 50 hard-task points on every model, while the tools alone are worth -9 to +15. What looked like an advantage of delivery was mostly knowledge: on DABStep, two facts missing from the manual, stated in words, recover most of the contract's lead over the pasted manual on two models, including on tasks whose transcripts nobody read; once both state the same facts, the contract's format adds a few points at most. A contract is worth what its author could determine. Compiled from documentation, it is worth the documentation on both benchmarks; on LiveSQLBench, knowledge helps nearly three times as much where the documentation determines the gold; an author who can query the data recovers what the data settles and misses the convention it cannot reveal. Delivery buys other things. On constructed questions at the ceiling, the contract in the prompt loses no accuracy up to four times a database's size, but looking it up as needed is cheaper from twice that size and runs past the context window; the lookup log traces a wrong answer to the definition behind it; and no governed run corrupted its database copy, against 50 ungoverned ones. This version revises our preprint, which credited the contract's lead to delivery.
