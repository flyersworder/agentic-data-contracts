Context layers, curated documentation that an analytics agent fetches at query time, produce large accuracy gains on text-to-SQL benchmarks, but a with/without comparison cannot say which part of the layer does the work. Using a data contract as the instrument, we separate the knowledge a layer carries from the tools and format that deliver it, on DABStep and LiveSQLBench, across seven models, with 53 predictions committed before the results they predict. The knowledge does the work: emptying the contract's descriptions and SQL definitions while keeping its tools and rules costs 26 to 50 points of accuracy on DABStep's hard tasks, nearly all of which turn on its fee procedure, on every model that ran it, while the tools alone are worth -9 to +15. Most of what looked like an advantage of delivery was knowledge: two facts missing from DABStep's manual, stated in words, recover most of the contract's lead over the pasted manual, also on held-out tasks; stated alike, the contract's representation adds about 4 to 6 points on the rule-matching tasks. A contract pays where it states something true that the documentation leaves out. A model that checks its definitions against the data recovers what the data settles, and a convention only when the gold uses the default idiom; on LiveSQLBench, whose documentation leaves a result-changing choice open on most tasks, no author we tried gained detectably. Delivery buys other things. On questions that name the metric they need, a contract four times a database's size costs the prompt no accuracy, but on GPT models looking definitions up is cheaper from twice that size and works past the context window, and the lookup log traces a wrong answer to the definition behind it. No run whose prompt stated the forbidden operations, through the tools or as text, corrupted its database copy: none of 29,356, against 50 of 21,790 without them.
