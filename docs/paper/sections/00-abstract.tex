\emph{Context layers}, curated documentation that an analytics agent
fetches at query time, produce large accuracy gains on text-to-SQL
benchmarks, but a with/without comparison cannot say which part of the
layer does the work. Using a data contract as the instrument, we separate
the knowledge a layer carries from the tools and format that deliver it,
on DABStep and LiveSQLBench, across seven models, with 53 predictions
fixed before their runs. The knowledge does the work: emptying the
contract's descriptions and SQL definitions while keeping its tools and
rules costs 26 to 50 points of accuracy on DABStep's hard tasks, nearly
all of which turn on its fee procedure, on every model that ran it, while
the tools alone are worth $-9$ to $+15$. Most of what looked like an
advantage of delivery was knowledge: two facts missing from DABStep's
manual, stated in words, recover most of the contract's lead over the
pasted manual, also on held-out tasks; stated alike, the contract's format
adds about 4 to 6 points on the rule-matching tasks. A contract pays where
it states something true that the documentation leaves out, and an author
can state only what the documentation or the data settles: a model that
checks its definitions against the data recovers what the data settles and
misses conventions it cannot reveal, and on LiveSQLBench, whose
documentation leaves most of the gold's choices open, no author we tried
gained detectably. Delivery buys other things. On questions that name the
metric they need, a contract four times a database's size costs the prompt
no accuracy, but looking definitions up is cheaper from twice that size
and works past the context window, and the lookup log traces a wrong
answer to the definition behind it. No run whose prompt stated the
forbidden operations, through the tools or as text, corrupted its database
copy: none of 29{,}356, against 50 of 21{,}790 without them.
