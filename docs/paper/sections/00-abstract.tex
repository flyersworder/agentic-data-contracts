Retrieval-augmented ``context layers'' --- curated domain documentation an
analytics agent fetches at query time --- are reported to produce large accuracy
gains on text-to-SQL benchmarks. Published results compare a system with a
context layer against one without, and from two accuracy numbers no mechanism
can be recovered: the gain might come from the semantic content, from the
retrieval scaffolding that delivers it, or from the pre-computed views and
macros that usually accompany it.

We report a controlled four-arm ablation on DABStep, run twice on two model
families. A fourth arm, \armH{}, is derived mechanically from the same frozen
artifact with every field of prose emptied and the tool surface, retrieval
instruction, table allow-list and operation rules held byte-for-byte fixed,
isolating the scaffolding from the semantics.

Contract-delivered semantics raise hard-task accuracy from 13.9\% to 55.1\% on
one model and from 22.6\% to 56.6\% on the other, beating the same knowledge
pasted verbatim into the prompt in both cases ($p{=}2{\times}10^{-17}$ and
$p{=}0.0067$, paired McNemar). \textbf{The scaffolding contributes nothing
measurable}: \armH{} is statistically indistinguishable from a bare schema on
both models ($p{=}0.058$ and $p{=}0.61$; pooled $p{=}0.29$), and the two runs
disagree on the sign.

A second result falls out of the same transcripts. The ungoverned arms
submitted 144 mutating SQL statements across 24 tasks; both governed arms
submitted zero, on both models ($p{=}6{\times}10^{-8}$). Because a rejected call
would still appear in the transcript, zero means the agents never attempted ---
the declared rules deterred rather than intercepted.

We also find that the advantage over a hand-written prompt shrinks as the base
model strengthens, report the negative result that the effect does not appear
outside the domain the contract describes, and release the harness, the
digest-pinned artifact, all 3{,}208 transcripts and the raw results.
