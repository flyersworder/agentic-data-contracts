\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An analytics agent pointed at a warehouse it does not understand fails in a
characteristic way. It reads the schema, writes plausible SQL, and returns a
number that is wrong for a reason no column name records: a code whose values
mean something specific to the business, a join whose cardinality depends on a
convention, a \texttt{NULL} that means \emph{applies to everything} rather than
\emph{unknown}. The query runs. The answer is confidently wrong.

The industry's answer is the \emph{context layer}: curated documentation stored
alongside the data and fetched by the agent at query time. Recent reports are
striking. MotherDuck's ``Guides'' raise DABStep accuracy by 72 percentage
points while cutting cost by roughly 55\%~\cite{motherduck-guides}. Similar
claims accompany every semantic-layer product now shipping.

These results share a shape: system-with-layer against system-without-layer,
two accuracy numbers, a large difference. That design establishes \emph{that}
context layers work. It cannot establish \emph{which part} works. And the part matters, because
the parts have wildly different costs. A context layer as
deployed bundles at least three things:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Semantic content} --- prose describing what the columns mean,
      how the metrics are defined, which conventions hold.
\item \textbf{Retrieval scaffolding} --- the tools that fetch it, the
      instruction to fetch before writing SQL, and the narrowed table
      surface that comes with a curated catalogue.
\item \textbf{Pre-computed artifacts} --- views and macros that encode a
      metric once so the agent calls it instead of deriving it.
\end{enumerate}

The three have different economics. Semantic content is written once per domain
and tends to exist already, in a vendor manual or a data dictionary.
Scaffolding is engineering, paid once. Pre-computed macros are paid \emph{per
metric}, forever, and only cover metrics somebody anticipated. A practitioner
deciding where to spend needs to know which of the three bought the 72 points.

Prior work has isolated the first. BIRD withholds and supplies human-written
evidence, worth 20 points to GPT-4~\cite{bird}; KaggleDBQA reports that
database documentation doubles parser accuracy~\cite{kaggledbqa}; an ontology
layer over an enterprise SQL schema moves GPT-4 from 16\% to
54\%~\cite{sequeda-kg}. What none of them separates is content from the
\emph{scaffolding that delivers it}: the tools, the retrieval instruction and
the narrowed table surface that arrive with any curated catalogue, and that a
with/without comparison necessarily bundles together.

\subsection*{Contribution}

We report a controlled ablation that separates the first two, and we are
explicit that we do not attempt the third (Section~\ref{sec:related} explains
why, and why that makes our absolute numbers lower and our question harder).

The design turns on a fourth arm. \armH{} takes the same frozen contract the
treatment arm uses and empties every field of prose (domain summaries, metric
descriptions, the SQL expression that carries the fee formula, column and
relationship descriptions) while leaving names, types, structure, the table
allow-list, the forbidden-operation rules and all nine governed tools
byte-for-byte identical. The agent still receives the instruction to look things
up, still has the tool, and still calls it. It is simply told nothing when it
does. Everything the deflationary reading attributes the gain to is present in
\armH{}; only the semantics are gone.

Four findings follow, and one non-finding.

\textbf{The content dominates, and the scaffolding is a real but secondary
effect} (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). \armH{} sits level with the bare-schema
baseline on one model, $+5.4$ points above it on another and $+14.2$ on the
third. Every model puts the
content step well above the scaffolding step, which is what the fourth arm was
built to test. But the stronger claim, that the scaffolding contributes
nothing, is false: two models supported it, an earlier version of this paper
made it, and the third model overturns it at $p{=}4{\times}10^{-7}$ against a
pooled null of $p{=}0.29$. That is a caution about how far two models
generalise.

\textbf{Delivery matters as much as possession}
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). Both the contract arm and the prompt arm are
given the governing rule; only the contract arm applies it. The difference is
where the rule sits: reachable by one targeted lookup, or buried in a wall of
text. Reading the SQL the agents actually submitted turns this from an
inference into an observation (Section~\ref{sec:clauses}): on tasks needing
all six of the contract's load-bearing clauses, the contract arm writes every
one of them 39\%, 65\% and 98\% of the time against the prompt arm's 3\%, 9\%
and 4\%.

\textbf{Capability arrives unevenly across the two kinds of task}
(Section~\ref{sec:derivation}). The contract compiles: transcribing its
declared SQL expressions into two views reproduces gold on 176 of 176 covered
tasks, so on those tasks the information is provably sufficient and any
shortfall is a failure to derive. That shortfall --- the \emph{derivation gap}
--- is 39.2, 37.3 and \textbf{5.1} points across the three models, while on
tasks that compose the same semantics further it moves only from 54.1 to 43.9.
The same capability increment closes 87\% of the first and 19\% of the second.
This is the finding with the most consequence for the economics above, and we
recorded it as a prediction in the public artifact repository before the third
run executed.

\textbf{Governance is measurable and nearly free}
(Section~\ref{sec:governance}), on models that need governing. Ungoverned arms
submitted 144 mutating statements across 24 tasks; governed arms submitted
none, and never attempted; the number measures deterrence by the declared
rules, not blocking by the enforcement layer behind them. On the strongest model no arm attempted a write at
all, governed or not: the hazard disappeared before the mechanism could be
tested on it.

The non-finding: on the 38, 30 and 38 hard tasks that fall outside the domain
the contract describes, the contract holds no advantage over a hand-written
prompt, and
on the strongest model the bare-schema baseline leads that slice outright. The
gain is domain-specific by construction. That is what a domain contract should
do, and also the sharpest limit on what one benchmark can show.
