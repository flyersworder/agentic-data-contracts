\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An analytics agent pointed at a warehouse it does not understand fails in a
characteristic way. It reads the schema, writes plausible SQL, and returns a
number that is wrong for a reason no column name records: a code whose values
mean something specific to the business, a join whose cardinality depends on a
convention, a \texttt{NULL} that means \emph{applies to everything} rather than
\emph{unknown}. The query runs. The answer is confidently wrong.

The industry's answer is the \emph{context layer}: curated documentation
stored alongside the data and fetched by the agent at query time. The reported
gains are large. MotherDuck's Guides raise DABStep accuracy by 72 percentage
points while cutting cost by roughly 55\%~\cite{motherduck-guides}, and
similar claims accompany every semantic-layer and context-layer product now
shipping.

These results share a shape: system with the layer against system without
it, two accuracy numbers, a large difference. That establishes \emph{that}
context layers work. It cannot establish \emph{which part} works, and the part
matters because the parts cost different amounts. A context layer as deployed
bundles at least three things:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Knowledge}: descriptions of what the columns mean, how
      the metrics are defined and which conventions hold, in words and
      often as SQL.
\item \textbf{Retrieval scaffolding}: the tools that fetch that knowledge, the
      instruction to fetch it before writing SQL, and the narrowed table
      surface that comes with a curated catalogue.
\item \textbf{Pre-computed artifacts}: views and macros that encode a metric
      once, so the agent calls it instead of deriving it.
\end{enumerate}

Knowledge is written once per domain and often exists already, in a
vendor manual or a data dictionary. Scaffolding is engineering, paid once.
Pre-computed macros are paid \emph{per metric}, forever, and cover only the
metrics somebody anticipated. A practitioner deciding where to spend needs to
know which of the three bought the 72 points.

Prior work has measured parts of this. BIRD withholds and supplies
human-written evidence, worth 20 points to GPT-4~\cite{bird}, and
documentation and ontologies raise accuracy
elsewhere~\cite{kaggledbqa,sequeda-kg}. Long-context studies deliver the
same schema and documentation whole or retrieved, and find long context as
accurate and retrieval cheaper~\cite{chung-long-context,death-schema-linking,rag-or-long-context}.
These compare amounts or deliveries of the same material. None removes the
knowledge while holding its delivery fixed, asks whether the documentation
determines the answer at all, or asks who wrote it.

\subsection*{What we did, and what we found}

We separate the parts with a \emph{data contract} as the instrument: a YAML
artifact that carries a domain's definitions together with the rules an
agent's tools enforce, and that can be emptied of its knowledge, delivered
in the prompt or behind lookup tools, and written by different authors
(Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). We use two benchmarks because they differ in
the property that turns out to matter. DABStep's documentation, read
closely together with its data, settles its answers; LiveSQLBench's, by
the consensus of two raters, settles fewer than half. Questions we constructed on
three LiveSQLBench databases add a third test bed whose knowledge is
complete by design. We ran seven models, three of them repeated three
times. Most experiments fixed numbered predictions in a commit before they
ran: 53 in all, of which 20 failed. Four findings.

\textbf{Knowledge does the work} (Section~\ref{sec:knowledge}). Emptying
the contract's descriptions and SQL definitions while keeping its tools,
instruction and rules drops DABStep hard-task accuracy by 26 to 50 points
on all six models that ran it, concentrated on the tasks that need the
fee procedure; the tools and instruction alone are worth
between $-9$ and $+15$ points, depending on the model. On LiveSQLBench,
agents with the documentation solve 60\% of the tasks it settles and 14\%
of those it clearly leaves open.

\textbf{What looked like delivery was knowledge}
(Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). On DABStep the contract beats the same
domain's manual, pasted into the prompt, by 20 to 23 hard-task points. The
natural reading, and the one an earlier version of this paper gave, is
delivery: the rule one lookup away rather than buried in a long prompt. Controlled experiments say otherwise. Two
facts the manual leaves out, stated in words, recover most of the gap,
including on held-out tasks whose transcripts were never inspected. Once
the prose and the contract state the same facts, the contract's representation adds
about 4 to 6 points on the tasks that match payments to fee rules, and looking a
rule up as the query is written adds a few points on some fee families.

\textbf{What a contract is worth depends on what its author could
determine} (Section~\ref{sec:authoring}). A model that compiles the
documentation entry by entry, checking each definition against the data,
yields the documentation plus what those checks reveal: on LiveSQLBench,
the KB; on DABStep, with a stronger compiler whose data checks surfaced the
empty-list wildcard and the overlap of fee rules, 90\% of the way from the first compile to the original contract. An author who can
query the data recovers what the data settles and two of the three gaps it
only suggests, misses a convention the data cannot reveal, and the contract
then carries exactly that knowledge. DABStep's summing of overlapping fees
is such a convention; the contracts got it right because the gold uses
the default join-and-sum. On LiveSQLBench, where the gold
settles task by task what the documentation leaves open, no author we tried
gained detectably.

\textbf{Delivery buys cost, scale and accountability; runs that state
the rules do not write} (Section~\ref{sec:buys}). On constructed questions that name the
metric they need, padding the contract with unrelated databases to four
times its size costs the prompt no accuracy, in the easier regime of
long-context studies~\cite{nolima,context-rot}. But on the two GPT models the lookup
arm is cheaper from twice the size, 1.7 to 2.4 times cheaper at four
times, and runs where the prompt no longer fits the context window. Its
lookups name the definition behind each answer, which locates the source of
a wrong one. And across every DABStep run, none of 29{,}356 runs whose
prompt stated the forbidden operations, in the contract's tools or its
text, corrupted its copy of the database, against 50 of 21{,}790 whose
prompt did not.

\textbf{What a practitioner should take from this.}
\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0pt
\item Write the knowledge down completely, with the data open, and state
      every convention a reader could get wrong: wildcards, how
      overlapping rules combine, units, how a \texttt{NULL} counts.
\item Do not expect a format, a retrieval loop or a stronger model to
      supply what the documentation does not determine.
\item For one database the prompt is the simplest and, with caching, the
      cheapest delivery; past about twice that, look definitions up and
      keep the lookup log.
\item State the forbidden operations, and serve the agent through a
      read-only role regardless.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions.} (1)~A decomposition of a context layer into
knowledge and scaffolding, on two benchmarks and seven models; (2)~a
revised account of delivery; (3)~determinability as the bound on what any
layer or author can add; (4)~measurements of what delivery buys; (5)~the
harness, contracts, predictions and results.
