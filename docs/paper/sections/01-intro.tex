\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An analytics agent pointed at a warehouse it does not understand fails in a
characteristic way. It reads the schema, writes plausible SQL, and returns a
number that is wrong for a reason no column name records: a code whose values
mean something specific to the business, a join whose cardinality depends on a
convention, a \texttt{NULL} that means \emph{applies to everything} rather than
\emph{unknown}. The query runs. The answer is confidently wrong.

The industry's answer is the \emph{context layer}: curated documentation
stored alongside the data and fetched by the agent at query time. The reported
gains are large. MotherDuck's Guides raise DABStep accuracy by 72 percentage
points while cutting cost by roughly 55\%~\cite{motherduck-guides}, and
similar claims accompany every semantic-layer and context-layer product now
shipping.

These results share a shape: system with the layer against system without
it, two accuracy numbers, a large difference. That establishes \emph{that}
context layers work. It cannot establish \emph{which part} works, and the part
matters because the parts cost different amounts. A context layer as deployed
bundles at least three things:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Semantic content}: prose describing what the columns mean, how
      the metrics are defined and which conventions hold.
\item \textbf{Retrieval scaffolding}: the tools that fetch that content, the
      instruction to fetch it before writing SQL, and the narrowed table
      surface that comes with a curated catalogue.
\item \textbf{Pre-computed artifacts}: views and macros that encode a metric
      once, so the agent calls it instead of deriving it.
\end{enumerate}

Semantic content is written once per domain and often exists already, in a
vendor manual or a data dictionary. Scaffolding is engineering, paid once.
Pre-computed macros are paid \emph{per metric}, forever, and cover only the
metrics somebody anticipated. A practitioner deciding where to spend needs to
know which of the three bought the 72 points.

Prior work has isolated the first. BIRD withholds and supplies human-written
evidence, worth 20 points to GPT-4~\cite{bird}; KaggleDBQA reports that
database documentation doubles parser accuracy~\cite{kaggledbqa}; an ontology
layer over an enterprise schema moves GPT-4 from 16\% to
54\%~\cite{sequeda-kg}. None separates content from the scaffolding that
delivers it, and none says what pre-computing the same content would have
bought.

\subsection*{What we did, and what we found}

We separate the parts with a \emph{data contract} as the instrument: a YAML
artifact that carries a domain's semantics together with the rules an
agent's tools enforce, and that can be emptied of its prose, compiled into
views, delivered in the prompt or behind tools, and written by different
authors. We run it on two benchmarks with documentation, DABStep and
LiveSQLBench, across seven models, three of them repeated three times.
After the first four-arm runs, every experiment fixed its predictions in a
commit before it ran: 53 in all, of which 20 failed. Four findings.

\textbf{Knowledge does the work} (Section~\ref{sec:knowledge}). Removing
the contract's prose while keeping its tools, instruction and rules drops
hard-task accuracy by 26 to 50 points on every model; the tools and
instruction without the prose are worth between $-9$ and $+15$, depending
on the model. On LiveSQLBench the documentation helps nearly three times
as much where it determines the gold as where it does not.

\textbf{What looked like delivery was knowledge}
(Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). The contract beats the same domain's manual
pasted into the prompt by 16 to 23 points. Our preprint attributed that to
delivery: the rule one lookup away rather than buried in a long prompt. A
sequence of controlled experiments says otherwise. Two facts the manual
leaves out, stated in words, recover most of the gap; once both sides state
the facts alike, the contract's form adds 4 to 6 points, and the governed
tools add a rule put in front of the agent at the moment it writes the
query.

\textbf{Where the knowledge comes from decides what it is worth}
(Section~\ref{sec:authoring}). A contract compiled entry by entry from the
documentation is worth what the documentation is, on both benchmarks. On
DABStep a better compile closes 90\% of the gap to the hand-written
contract, because the knowledge is determined by the manual and the data;
on LiveSQLBench no author gains, because the gold settles what the
documentation leaves open, task by task. An author who can query the data
recovers what the data settles, two thirds of what it only suggests, and
none of the conventions it cannot reveal, and the contract then carries
exactly that knowledge.

\textbf{Delivery buys cost, scale, accountability and governance, not
accuracy} (Section~\ref{sec:buys}). Grown from one database to 22, the
contract in the prompt loses no accuracy up to four times its size, but
the governed contract is cheaper from twice its size, 1.7 to 2.4 times
cheaper at four times, and runs where the prompt no longer fits the
context window. Its lookups name the definition behind each answer, which
locates the source of a wrong one. And across every DABStep run, the
ungoverned arms corrupted their working copy of the database 50 times and
the governed arms never.

\textbf{What a practitioner should take from this.} Write the knowledge
down completely, with the data open, and state the conventions a reader
could get wrong; that is where the accuracy is. Do not expect a format or
a retrieval loop to supply what the documentation does not determine. For
one database the prompt is the simplest and, with caching, the cheapest
delivery; as the contract grows past about twice that, look things up as
needed, and keep the lookup log. Pre-compute a metric only where agents
demonstrably fail to derive it.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1)~The decomposition of a context layer into
content and scaffolding, on two benchmarks and seven models.
(2)~A revised account of delivery, with the experiments that revised it,
including our own preprint's. (3)~An authoring result that ties a
contract's value to whether its knowledge is determinable. (4)~Measurements
of what delivery buys: cost and scale, accountability, governance.
(5)~The harness, contracts, compilers, predictions and results, released
with the paper.
