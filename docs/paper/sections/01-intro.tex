\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An analytics agent pointed at a warehouse it does not understand fails in a
characteristic way. It reads the schema, writes plausible SQL, and returns a
number that is wrong for a reason no column name records: a code whose values
mean something specific to the business, a join whose cardinality depends on a
convention, a \texttt{NULL} that means \emph{applies to everything} rather than
\emph{unknown}. The query runs. The answer is confidently wrong.

The industry's answer is the \emph{context layer}: curated documentation
stored alongside the data and fetched by the agent at query time. The reported
gains are large. MotherDuck's Guides raise DABStep accuracy by 72 percentage
points while cutting cost by roughly 55\%~\cite{motherduck-guides}, and
similar claims accompany every semantic-layer and context-layer product now
shipping.

These results share a shape: system with the layer against system without
it, two accuracy numbers, a large difference. That establishes \emph{that}
context layers work. It cannot establish \emph{which part} works, and the part
matters because the parts cost different amounts. A context layer as deployed
bundles at least three things:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Knowledge}: descriptions of what the columns mean, how
      the metrics are defined and which conventions hold, in words and
      often as SQL.
\item \textbf{Retrieval scaffolding}: the tools that fetch that knowledge, the
      instruction to fetch it before writing SQL, and the narrowed table
      surface of a curated catalogue.
\item \textbf{Pre-computed artifacts}: views and macros that encode a metric
      once, so the agent calls it instead of deriving it.
\end{enumerate}

Knowledge is written once per domain and often exists already, in a
vendor manual or a data dictionary. Scaffolding is engineering, paid once.
Pre-computed macros are paid \emph{per metric}, forever, and cover only the
metrics somebody anticipated. A practitioner deciding where to spend needs to
know which of the three bought the 72 points.

Prior work has measured parts of this. BIRD withholds and supplies
human-written evidence, worth 20 points to GPT-4~\cite{bird}, and
documentation and ontologies raise accuracy
elsewhere~\cite{kaggledbqa,sequeda-kg}. Long-context studies deliver the
same schema and documentation whole or retrieved, and find long context as
accurate and retrieval cheaper~\cite{chung-long-context,death-schema-linking,rag-or-long-context}.
These compare amounts or deliveries of the same material. None removes the
knowledge while holding its delivery fixed, or holds the knowledge equal
while varying its delivery, so none can tell a delivery effect from
knowledge one layer states and the other omits.

\subsection*{What we did, and what we found}

We separate the parts with a \emph{data contract} as the instrument: a YAML
artifact that keeps a domain's semantics explicit and apart from the tools
that serve and enforce them, so that it can be emptied of its knowledge,
given extra facts in identical words, and delivered in the prompt or behind
lookup tools (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). It is a convenient instrument for
this, not the only possible one. On DABStep~\cite{dabstep} we apply all
three manipulations; on questions we constructed over three
LiveSQLBench~\cite{livesqlbench} databases, the notes and the routes; on
LiveSQLBench itself, the routes. We ran seven models, three of them
repeated three times, and fixed 57 predictions in commits before
their runs, of which 22 failed.

\textbf{Knowledge, not scaffolding} (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}).
Emptying the contract's descriptions and SQL definitions while keeping its
tools, instruction and rules costs 26 to 50 DABStep hard-task points on
six models, nearly all of it in the fee procedure. The empty scaffolding is
worth $-9$ to $+15$, and at most $+0.2$ on the repeated panels.

\textbf{Two omitted data semantics} (Sections~\ref{sec:panel-gaps}
to~\ref{sec:plain}). The contract leads the manual pasted into the prompt
by 20 to 43 hard-task points on the three repeated models (14 to 45 on
the four single runs), which looks like delivery. On GPT-6 luna and
Qwen~3.8 it is mostly two pieces of data semantics the manual omits. The
manual says a null fee field means ``all''; the data stores that as an
empty list. And when several fee rules match one payment their fees add
up, which the manual never says. Stated in prose, the two recover most of
the lead on luna and, end-to-end, all of it on Qwen, whose prose with both
facts outscores the governed contract. The second lifts accuracy by 19 and
34 points on tasks outside the transcripts we read to find it. How plainly
they are stated matters too: on Qwen the prose beats the contract's own
text, which carries both semantics less plainly, by 16.6 points.

\textbf{Delivery at equal knowledge} (Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). With the
two semantics stated alike, the contract's text leads prose on the
rule-matching tasks by 3.7 and 5.5 points, provisionally: under exact
matching luna's lead falls to 1.8. Looked up or pasted, the contract shows
no advantage end-to-end once both arms state the semantics; on Qwen's
official scoring the lookup leads, largely because its answers more often
take the requested format. On LiveSQLBench the route makes no detectable
difference.

\textbf{Secondary results} (Sections~\ref{sec:authoring}
and~\ref{sec:buys}). A compiler or author with the data open recovered
encodings the data shows, and what it got wrong travelled through prose
and contract alike; the summing convention is settled by neither the
documentation nor the data. By gold-aware model raters, LiveSQLBench's
documentation leaves a result-changing choice open on 132 of 302 tasks
(112 to 146 across rater pairings). At four times a database's size,
looking definitions up is 1.7 to 2.4 times cheaper than pasting the
contract's YAML on the GPT models, and about 1.1 times cheaper than a prose
KB; the lookups leave a log of the definitions each answer consulted. No
run whose prompt stated the forbidden operations corrupted its database
copy (0 of 36{,}574, against 50 of 21{,}790), an association rather than a
test of enforcement.

\textbf{What a practitioner should take from this.}
\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0pt
\item Before crediting a context layer's machinery, check that the
      comparison holds its semantics equal.
\item Write down the semantics a reader could misread (wildcard encodings,
      what happens when several rules match, units, \texttt{NULL}s), plainly,
      and check them against the data.
\item Where accuracy is comparable on your models and tasks, choose the
      delivery for cost, capacity and the consultation log; a prose KB in
      the prompt held its accuracy at 22 databases' worth, but used about
      seven times the input of looking the contract up.
\item State the forbidden operations, and serve the agent through a
      read-only role regardless.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions.} (1)~The finding: on DABStep an apparent delivery
advantage of a context layer is mostly two omitted data semantics,
supported by a six-model knowledge control and, on two models, a held-out
lift. (2)~A knowledge-equal protocol: the hollow control, facts as
identical notes, and the same knowledge by two routes, read as conditional
contrasts rather than an additive decomposition. (3)~Benchmark properties:
DABStep's manual omits both semantics; by model raters, LiveSQLBench's KB
leaves 132 of 302 primary tasks open. (4)~The harness, contracts, result
rows, predictions and transcripts.
