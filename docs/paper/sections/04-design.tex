\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Two benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

\textbf{DABStep}~\cite{dabstep} asks financial-analytics questions over one
payments warehouse, with a vendor manual as its documentation. Most hard
tasks turn on one procedure: matching each payment to the fee rules that
apply to it, with wildcards, monthly volume and fraud bands, and a fee
formula. We use the 401 of its 450 test tasks for which we reconstruct a
gold (332 hard, 69 easy; Section~\ref{sec:grading}).

\textbf{LiveSQLBench}~\cite{livesqlbench} (Base-Full v1) asks SQL
questions over 22 databases in as many domains, each shipped with a
knowledge base (KB) of 30 to 96 entries that define its metrics, classes
and conventions. We port it from PostgreSQL to DuckDB: the grader runs an
answer's final SQL on a read-only copy with PostgreSQL's NULL ordering and
integer division, and compares the result with the frozen PostgreSQL gold.
The 302 \emph{primary} tasks are those whose gold reproduces in DuckDB;
73 more reproduce only as a set and are reported apart. Its
knowledge is mostly composite (dependent formulas, bands, a change of
grain) rather than a single procedure that many tasks share.

\textbf{Constructed questions.} Experiments 4, 4b and 6 need knowledge that
is complete by construction. On three LiveSQLBench databases from three
domains (returns logistics, labour certification, solar plants) each
received a rule-matching procedure and a composite, written as KB entries,
reimplemented from their text by an independent reviewer and answered by a
blind solver given only the KB: 60 questions in matched shapes, ten per
piece and database.

Every arm sees the same tasks, database, model, answer-format instruction
and scorer, and differs in what it knows and how that reaches the agent
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}). The \emph{plain tools} are three ungoverned ones
(\texttt{list\_tables}, \texttt{describe\_table}, \texttt{execute\_sql}).
The \emph{governed tools} are the contract's nine (Section~\ref{sec:tools}),
with a short system prompt naming the domains and metrics and instructing
the agent to look them up before writing SQL. \armM{} pastes the
benchmark's own documentation into the prompt: on DABStep the 24{,}177
characters of \texttt{manual.md}, on LiveSQLBench the database's KB. It is
the comparison a practitioner faces, since the alternative to a contract
is not ignorance but a long prompt. \armH{} is \armC{} with every field of
prose emptied, the control that turns the four arms into an ablation.

The second block exists because \armM{} and \armC{} were not
knowledge-equal: the contract's author had added facts from the data
(Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). Each note is written once, never reworded
between runs, and the text arms carry the contract's two files verbatim.

Three models carry the repeated experiments (Table~\ref{tab:models}):
\mSol{}, a frontier model; \mLuna{}, the small tier of its family on the
same deployment, which varies capability alone; and \mQwen{}, an
open-weight model served at FP8 with thinking on, temperature~0 and
seed~0, which varies vendor, family and serving stack. GPT-6 accepts only
its default temperature, so its repeats measure sampling variance as well
as the arms. Reasoning effort is \texttt{medium} for the GPT models. The
four single runs of the preprint, each pinned to one provider endpoint,
supply breadth across four more model families
(\appref{app:harness}); their \mDS{} run lost 29\% of its rows to rate
limiting and is reported on its 279-task complete-case set
(\appref{app:truncation}).

\subsection{Pre-registration and statistics}
\label{sec:prereg}

From the knowledge-or-delivery series on, every experiment fixed its arms,
its endpoints, its predictions and how each outcome would be read in a run
commit before the run began; a smoke run after that commit could not
change them, and every result row stamps the commit it ran on. The four single
runs had a pre-registered design and primary model but no numbered
predictions. Across both benchmarks, 53 predictions were fixed in 16 run
commits: 29 held, 3 held in part, 20 failed, and one was read as
equivalent, which its run commit registered as an outcome of its own
(\appref{app:predictions} lists them). A test is reported as fixed even
where its reading later proved too narrow, and analyses chosen after a
result was seen are labelled exploratory.

The unit is the task, scored by its correct runs over the repeats. Arms
are compared with sign tests over tasks, ties dropped: one-sided where a
direction was predicted, and required to hold in direction on each model
when pooled over models. Where a null was registered as a tie, it is read
by a $\pm$5-point bound, with bootstrap intervals over tasks where an
equivalence was registered. The four single runs use McNemar's exact test
over paired tasks~\cite{mcnemar,dietterich}. No multiplicity correction
is applied; Section~\ref{sec:threats} says what that exposes.

\subsection{Grading}
\label{sec:grading}
\label{sec:golds}

DABStep withholds its test golds. We reconstruct them from the
leaderboard's published per-task files by plurality over answers the
benchmark's grader marked correct (a 75\% threshold, median realised share
0.981), which yields 401 golds after excluding five consensus answers that
verification showed wrong (\appref{app:golds}). We then measured the
reconstruction: \armC{} and \armM{} on \mGLM{}, swept over all 450 tasks
and submitted, were graded by the leaderboard against the withheld golds
at 51.9\% and 18.8\% on the hard tasks; our golds run about 3 to 6 points
high, on both arms (\appref{app:leniency}). Answers are graded by
DABStep's own scorer, vendored verbatim. \mQwen{} often states its
working and then the answer as its last paragraph, which the scorer marks
wrong; the \emph{end-to-end} view grades that last paragraph with the same
scorer, never downgrades, and is reported beside the official verdicts on
\mQwen{}. On the GPT-6 models the two views agree. LiveSQLBench answers are
SQL, graded by its official Soft-EX rules, reproduced exactly.

\subsection{What is held fixed}
\label{sec:fixed}

Within a run everything but the arm is fixed: the row limit (50 rows per
result), the request limit, the token budget, the model and its settings,
the endpoint, the task set, the golds and the scorer. In each repeated
experiment the arms run together within each repeat and pair within it.
An agent that exhausts its budget is forced to a final answer rather than
discarded, and a run that ends in an error counts as wrong; a retry pass
reruns transient errors once. Harness defects found along the way are
recorded with the rows they affected (\appref{app:harness}).
