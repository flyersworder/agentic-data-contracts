\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Two benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

\textbf{DABStep}~\cite{dabstep} asks financial-analytics questions over one
payments warehouse, with a vendor manual as its documentation. Most hard
tasks turn on one procedure: matching each payment to the fee rules that
apply to it, with wildcards, monthly volume and fraud bands, and a fee
formula. We use the 401 of its 450 test tasks for which we reconstruct a
gold (332 hard, 69 easy; Section~\ref{sec:grading}). Three subsets recur.
The \emph{rule-matching} tasks (199) are those both procedural-load raters
(Section~\ref{sec:procedures}) marked as matching records against a rule
table, in practice the fee rules; the other 202 include the total-fee and
fee-average questions. A \emph{family} is the set of tasks one fee
computation answers, differing only in parameters such as merchant and
month; eleven families cover 176 of the 332 hard tasks.
\emph{Held-out} tasks are rule-matching tasks whose transcripts we never
inspected (Section~\ref{sec:second-fact}).

\textbf{LiveSQLBench}~\cite{livesqlbench} (Base-Full v1) asks SQL
questions over 22 databases in as many domains, each shipped with a
knowledge base (KB) of 30 to 96 entries that define its metrics, classes
and conventions. Of its 600 tasks, 411 are read-only queries; the rest
change data and are out of scope. We port it from PostgreSQL to DuckDB:
the grader runs an answer's final SQL on a read-only copy with
PostgreSQL's NULL ordering and integer division, and compares the result
with the frozen PostgreSQL gold. Executing each gold query 20 times in
DuckDB, 302 reproduce exactly (the \emph{primary} tasks), 73 reproduce only as a set
and are reported apart, and 36 are excluded, as not reproducible or
because they read the clock. Its knowledge is mostly composite (dependent
formulas, bands, a change of grain) rather than one procedure that many
tasks share.

\textbf{Constructed questions,} a third test bed. Three experiments
(Sections~\ref{sec:procedures}, \ref{sec:gaps} and~\ref{sec:scale}) need
knowledge that is complete by construction. We chose three LiveSQLBench databases from
three domains (returns logistics, labour certification, solar plants) and
wrote two new pieces of knowledge into each one's KB: a rule-matching
procedure and a composite. Separate Opus~5.5 sessions wrote the pieces
and the questions, reimplemented every piece from its text, and, given
only the KB, solved all 60 questions: ten per piece and database, in
matched shapes.

Every arm sees the same tasks, database, model, answer-format instruction
and scorer, and differs in what it knows and how that reaches the agent
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}, Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). The \emph{plain tools}
are three ungoverned ones (\texttt{list\_tables}, \texttt{describe\_table},
\texttt{execute\_sql}). The \emph{governed tools} are the contract's nine
(Section~\ref{sec:tools}): lookups that deliver the knowledge, and a
validator that enforces the rules, with a short system prompt naming the
domains and metrics and instructing the agent to look them up before
writing SQL. \armS{} gives the agent only the plain tools and the schema.
\armM{} pastes the benchmark's own documentation into the prompt: on
DABStep the 22{,}126 characters of \texttt{manual.md}, on LiveSQLBench the
database's KB. It is the comparison a practitioner faces, since the
alternative to a contract is not ignorance but a long prompt. \armC{} is
the original contract behind the governed tools, and \armH{} the same
with every description and SQL definition emptied, the control that turns
the four arms into an ablation.

The second block exists because \armM{} and \armC{} were not
knowledge-equal: the contract states two facts the manual does not
(Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). \armMw{} adds the first as a note (the
empty-list wildcard) and \armMws{} adds both (that the fees of
overlapping rules add up). \armT{} puts the contract's two files verbatim
in the prompt over the plain tools, and \armTws{} adds the same two notes
to it. Each note is written once and never reworded between runs. In the
third block, \armComp{} is a contract compiled from the documentation
(Section~\ref{sec:authoring-paths}), and \armMsql{} the documentation plus
every compiled metric's SQL. The gaps and scale experiments add arms of
their own, defined where they are used. Most experiments were designed from
what the one before found (Table~\ref{tab:experiments}).

Three models carry the repeated experiments (Table~\ref{tab:models}):
\mSol{}, a frontier model; \mLuna{}, the small tier of its family on the
same deployment, which varies capability alone; and \mQwen{}, an
open-weight model served at FP8 with thinking on, temperature~0 and
seed~0, which varies vendor, family and serving stack. The GPT-6 models
accept only their default temperature. Serving is not deterministic even
for \mQwen{}: between any two repeats 12.5 to 14.2\% of its task-arm
verdicts change (8.3 to 9.7\% on \mSol{}, about 12\% on \mLuna{}), so the
repeats measure variance on every model. Reasoning effort is
\texttt{medium} for the GPT models. The four single runs, each pinned to
one provider endpoint, supply breadth across four more model families
(\appref{app:harness}); their \mDS{} run lost 29\% of its rows to rate
limiting and is reported on its 279-task complete-case set
(\appref{app:truncation}).

\subsection{Pre-registration and statistics}
\label{sec:prereg}

From the one-fact experiment on, every experiment committed its arms,
endpoints and how each outcome would be read before its sweep, and most
also fixed numbered predictions; every result row stamps the commit it ran
on. Most commits came before any run or after a smoke run only. One,
LiveSQLBench's (6), came after Qwen's per-arm totals for two repeats were
seen, but before the subgroup statistic it predicts; its commit says so. The four single
runs had a pre-registered design and primary model but no numbered
predictions, and neither panel fixed any. In all, 53 predictions were
fixed in 15 commits: 29 held, 3 held in part, 20 failed, and one was read
as equivalent, an outcome its commit registered in advance
(\appref{app:predictions}). Analyses chosen after a result was seen are
labelled exploratory. Neither panel fixed numbered predictions, so the
headline decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} is a
pre-specified design without registered outcomes.

The unit is the task. With $k$ repeats a task scores 0 to $k$ for each
arm, its number of correct runs, and a comparison between two arms counts
the tasks on which each scores higher, dropping ties; we write ``205 tasks
to 12''. A sign test on those counts is exact if the two arms are
exchangeable within a task, so the variance between repeats is absorbed;
the four single runs use McNemar's exact test over paired
tasks~\cite{mcnemar,dietterich}. Tasks are not independent, however.
DABStep's tasks fall into families and question shapes, LiveSQLBench's
into 22 databases, and the constructed questions into six pieces of
knowledge (five gap clusters in the gaps experiment). Every effect is
therefore given in points with a 95\% bootstrap interval over those
clusters, and where a task-level test and its cluster-level counterpart
disagree, the text says so. In-text $p$-values are two-sided and quoted no
smaller than $10^{-10}$. A test fixed before its run carries its
prediction number, as in \emph{(28)}; any other test is exploratory and is
marked \emph{(expl.)}. The index records each registered verdict at its
registered sidedness, one-sided where a direction was predicted. A
registered tie holds when the gap is within 5 points and the sign test
gives $p \geq 0.10$; a registered equivalence when the interval of the
difference lies within $\pm$5 points. Accuracy is \emph{strict}: a run
that ends in an error or at the turn limit counts as wrong. No
multiplicity correction is applied; Section~\ref{sec:threats} says which
results would not survive one.

\subsection{Grading}
\label{sec:grading}
\label{sec:golds}

DABStep withholds its test golds. We reconstruct them from the
leaderboard's published per-task files: for each task, the answer that at
least 75\% of the submissions graded correct agree on (the median share is
0.981). That yields 401 golds after excluding five consensus answers that
verification showed wrong (\appref{app:golds}). We then measured the
reconstruction: \armC{} and \armM{} on \mGLM{}, swept over all 450 tasks
and submitted, were graded by the leaderboard against the withheld golds
at 51.9\% and 18.8\% on the hard tasks, and our golds run about 3 to 6
points high on the hard split, on both arms (\appref{app:leniency}).
Answers are graded by DABStep's own scorer,
vendored verbatim. \mQwen{} often states its working and then the answer
as its last paragraph, which the scorer marks wrong; the
\emph{end-to-end} view grades that last paragraph with the same scorer,
never downgrades, and is reported beside the official verdicts on
\mQwen{}. On the GPT-6 models the two views agree. LiveSQLBench answers
are SQL, graded by the benchmark's Soft-EX rules, which compare the
answer's result table with the gold's after normalising decimals, rounding
and \texttt{DISTINCT}. Our DuckDB port departs from them in three
documented ways, all fixed when the task set was frozen: JSON text is
parsed, nested dates are normalised, and numbers match within one
two-place rounding step (floats also within $10^{-5}$ relative), so that
every frozen gold grades as correct against itself. Matching one database
instance is not semantic equivalence~\cite{test-suites}; both benchmarks
grade that way.

\subsection{What is held fixed}
\label{sec:fixed}

Within a run everything but the arm is fixed: the row limit (50 rows per
result), the request limit, the token budget, the model and its settings,
the endpoint, the task set, the golds and the scorer. In most repeated
experiments the arms ran together within each repeat; where an arm ran
separately (\armMw{} on \mSol{}, \armMws{} on \mLuna{}), it pairs by task
and repeat. An agent that exhausts its budget is forced to a final answer
rather than discarded, and a retry pass reruns transient errors once.
Harness defects found along the way are recorded with the rows they
affected (\appref{app:harness}).
