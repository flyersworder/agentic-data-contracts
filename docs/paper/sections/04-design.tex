\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Two benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

\textbf{DABStep}~\cite{dabstep} asks financial-analytics questions over one
payments warehouse, with a vendor manual as its documentation. Most hard
tasks turn on one procedure: matching each payment to the fee rules that
apply to it, with wildcards, monthly volume and fraud bands, and a fee
formula. We use the 401 of its 450 test tasks for which we reconstruct a
gold (332 hard, 69 easy; Section~\ref{sec:grading}).

\textbf{LiveSQLBench}~\cite{livesqlbench} (Base-Full v1) asks SQL
questions over 22 databases in as many domains, each shipped with a
knowledge base (KB) of 30 to 96 entries that define its metrics, classes
and conventions. Of its 600 tasks, 411 are read-only queries; the rest
change data and are out of scope. We port it from PostgreSQL to DuckDB:
the grader runs an answer's final SQL on a read-only copy with
PostgreSQL's NULL ordering and integer division, and compares the result
with the frozen PostgreSQL gold. Run 20 times in DuckDB, 302 golds
reproduce exactly (the \emph{primary} tasks), 73 reproduce only as a set
and are reported apart, and 36 are excluded, as not reproducible or
because they read the clock. Its knowledge is mostly composite (dependent
formulas, bands, a change of grain) rather than one procedure that many
tasks share.

\textbf{Constructed questions.} Experiments 4, 4b and 6 need knowledge that
is complete by construction. We chose three LiveSQLBench databases from
three domains (returns logistics, labour certification, solar plants) and
wrote two new pieces of knowledge into each one's KB: a rule-matching
procedure and a composite. An independent reviewer reimplemented every
piece from its text, and a blind solver given only the KB answered all 60
questions: ten per piece and database, in matched shapes.

Every arm sees the same tasks, database, model, answer-format instruction
and scorer, and differs in what it knows and how that reaches the agent
(Table~\ref{tab:arms}). The \emph{plain tools} are three ungoverned ones
(\texttt{list\_tables}, \texttt{describe\_table}, \texttt{execute\_sql}).
The \emph{governed tools} are the contract's nine (Section~\ref{sec:tools}),
with a short system prompt naming the domains and metrics and instructing
the agent to look them up before writing SQL. \armM{} pastes the
benchmark's own documentation into the prompt: on DABStep the 24{,}177
characters of \texttt{manual.md}, on LiveSQLBench the database's KB. It is
the comparison a practitioner faces, since the alternative to a contract
is not ignorance but a long prompt. \armH{} is \armC{} with every field of
prose emptied, the control that turns the four arms into an ablation.

The second block exists because \armM{} and \armC{} were not
knowledge-equal: the contract states two facts the manual does not
(Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). \armMw{} adds the first as a note
(\emph{w}, the empty-list wildcard) and \armMws{} adds both (\emph{s},
that overlapping fee rules each apply). Each note is written once and
never reworded between runs, and the text arms carry the contract's two
files verbatim. Table~\ref{tab:experiments} lists the experiments; most
were designed from what the one before found.

Three models carry the repeated experiments (Table~\ref{tab:models}):
\mSol{}, a frontier model; \mLuna{}, the small tier of its family on the
same deployment, which varies capability alone; and \mQwen{}, an
open-weight model served at FP8 with thinking on, temperature~0 and
seed~0, which varies vendor, family and serving stack. The GPT-6 models
accept only their default temperature. Serving is not deterministic even
for \mQwen{}: between any two repeats 12.5 to 14.2\% of its task-arm
verdicts change (8.3 to 9.7\% on \mSol{}, about 12\% on \mLuna{}), so the
repeats measure variance on every model. Reasoning effort is
\texttt{medium} for the GPT models. The four single runs, each pinned to
one provider endpoint, supply breadth across four more model families
(\appref{app:harness}); their \mDS{} run lost 29\% of its rows to rate
limiting and is reported on its 279-task complete-case set
(\appref{app:truncation}).

\subsection{Pre-registration and statistics}
\label{sec:prereg}

From the one-fact experiment on, every experiment committed its arms,
endpoints and how each outcome would be read before it ran, and most also
fixed numbered predictions; a smoke run after that commit could not change
them, and every result row stamps the commit it ran on. The four single
runs had a pre-registered design and primary model but no numbered
predictions, and neither panel fixed any. In all, 53 predictions were
fixed in 15 commits: 29 held, 3 held in part, 20 failed, and one was read
as equivalent, an outcome its commit registered in advance
(\appref{app:predictions}). Analyses chosen after a result was seen are
labelled exploratory.

The unit is the task. With $k$ repeats a task scores 0 to $k$ for each
arm, its number of correct runs, and a comparison between two arms counts
the tasks on which each scores higher, dropping ties. Arms are compared by
sign tests on those counts: one-sided where a direction was predicted, and
required to hold in direction on each model when pooled over models.
Where a null was registered as a tie, it is read by a $\pm$5-point bound,
with bootstrap intervals over tasks where an equivalence was registered.
The four single runs use McNemar's exact test over paired
tasks~\cite{mcnemar,dietterich}. Accuracy is \emph{strict}: a run that
ends in an error or at the turn limit counts as wrong. No multiplicity
correction is applied; Section~\ref{sec:threats} says what that exposes.

\subsection{Grading}
\label{sec:grading}
\label{sec:golds}

DABStep withholds its test golds. We reconstruct them from the
leaderboard's published per-task files: for each task, the answer that at
least 75\% of the submissions graded correct agree on (the median share is
0.981). That yields 401 golds after excluding five consensus answers that
verification showed wrong (\appref{app:golds}). We then measured the
reconstruction: \armC{} and \armM{} on \mGLM{}, swept over all 450 tasks
and submitted, were graded by the leaderboard against the withheld golds
at 51.9\% and 18.8\% on the hard tasks, and our golds run about 3 to 6
points high on the hard split, on both arms (\appref{app:leniency}). The
reconstructed golds are not redistributed; the script that rebuilds them
from the public files is. Answers are graded by DABStep's own scorer,
vendored verbatim. \mQwen{} often states its working and then the answer
as its last paragraph, which the scorer marks wrong; the
\emph{end-to-end} view grades that last paragraph with the same scorer,
never downgrades, and is reported beside the official verdicts on
\mQwen{}. On the GPT-6 models the two views agree. LiveSQLBench answers
are SQL, graded by the benchmark's Soft-EX rules, which compare the
answer's result table with the gold's after normalising decimals, rounding
and \texttt{DISTINCT}; we reproduce them exactly.

\subsection{What is held fixed}
\label{sec:fixed}

Within a run everything but the arm is fixed: the row limit (50 rows per
result), the request limit, the token budget, the model and its settings,
the endpoint, the task set, the golds and the scorer. In most repeated
experiments the arms ran together within each repeat; where an arm ran
separately (\armMw{} on \mSol{}, \armMws{} on \mLuna{}), it pairs by task
and repeat. An agent that exhausts its budget is forced to a final answer
rather than discarded, and a retry pass reruns transient errors once.
Harness defects found along the way are recorded with the rows they
affected (\appref{app:harness}).
