\section{What Looked Like Delivery Was Knowledge}
\label{sec:delivery}

The contract beats the manual by 16 to 23 points on DABStep
(Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}). Both carry the same domain, so the
preprint read the gap as delivery: the contract's rule is one lookup away
when the query is written, while the manual's is buried in a long prompt.
That reading does not survive the experiments in this section. Each
replaced one difference between the arms with a controlled one, in the
order the transcripts suggested it.

\subsection{One fact the manual leaves out}
\label{sec:one-fact}
\label{sec:interpretation}

The manual says a null fee field ``applies to all possible values''. The
fee data never uses null for its three list-typed fields; it stores ``all''
as an empty list, on 720 of 1{,}000 rules for \texttt{account\_type}. The
contract's author took that from the data and built it into the matching
predicate. \armMw{} is \armM{} plus that fact, once, in words and without
SQL; it was fixed with its predictions before its first run. On \mSol{} it
recovers 77\% of the gap between manual and contract (\armM{} 65.1\%,
\armMw{} 77.3\%, \armC{} 81.0\%; 90 tasks to 28 over \armM{}), on \mQwen{}
41\%, and on \mLuna{} 38\%. The fact acts where it should: on the three
families that only need the right set of fee rules, \armMw{} reaches the
contract on \mSol{} (180 of 180 task-runs against 42 for \armM{}). On the
three total-fee families it does nothing, and the contract still wins
there by 32 tasks to 0.

\armMw{} still trailed the contract by 29 points on \mLuna{}'s
rule-matching tasks: the 199 that match each payment against a table of
fee rules. The transcripts of the 34 tasks the contract's text solved and
\armMw{} missed show a second omission. All 34 add up the fees of many
transactions, and about 30 turn on what happens when several fee rules
match one transaction. The manual never says. The contract's total-fee
definition sums over every matched pair, which is the gold convention,
and does not mark that as an interpretation. The prose arm saw the
overlap and invented a one-rule policy; twice it computed the gold number
and rejected it as double counting.

\armMws{} adds that convention to \armMw{} as a second note. It lifts the
prose by 28.5 points on \mLuna{} (95 tasks to 4) and 42.7 on \mQwen{} (112
to 4). \textbf{Most of what the preprint called delivery was these two
facts.} With both stated, the prose ties the contract's text on \mLuna{}
($+0.8$ points, 26 tasks to 19, $p{=}0.37$).

\subsection{The facts stated alike}
\label{sec:alike}

On \mQwen{} the prose then led the contract's text by 16.6 points, which
the predictions did not foresee. The design was rechecked and one thing was
unequal: the prose states each fact as a note of its own, while the
contract states the summing convention less plainly, speaking of each
transaction ``joined to \emph{the} fee rule'' before saying to sum, and
places the empty-list clause 28{,}000 characters after a matching rule
that mentions null only. \mQwen{} often took ``the fee rule'' at its word: an approximate keyword
count finds single-rule reasoning in 103 of its 199 rule-matching runs on
the contract's text, against 37 on \mLuna{}.

\armTws{} is \armT{} plus the same two notes, word for word. With the
facts stated alike (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}), the contract's text leads the
prose on the rule-matching tasks by 3.7 points on \mLuna{} (32 tasks to
13, $p{=}0.007$) and 5.5 on \mQwen{} (35 to 10, $p{=}3{\times}10^{-4}$).
So the contract's form adds a modest gain beyond the facts, on the tasks
the procedure governs. One caveat bounds it: \armTws{} states each fact
twice, in the contract and in the note, and the prose once, so part of
the lead may be repetition rather than form.

\subsection{What the governed tools add}
\label{sec:tools-add}

\armT{} and \armC{} hold the same contract and differ only in delivery: the
files in the prompt over plain tools, or the governed lookups. On
\mLuna{}'s rule-matching tasks they tie ($-0.7$ points, 15 tasks to 18).
On the other 202 tasks the governed arm leads by 5 points (38 tasks to 15,
$p{=}0.002$), most of it in three families that average fees over the
rule table (33 tasks to 4 there). Every one of the 50 runs there that the governed arm solved and the
text arm missed fails the same way in the text arm: its answer equals, to
six decimals, the result of dropping the wildcard rules. The text arm had
the rule three times in its 60{,}000-character prompt. The governed arm
got it from a call made just before its first fee query: the matching
metric's lookup in 34 runs, \texttt{lookup\_domain} or the governed
\texttt{describe\_table} in 16. \texttt{inspect\_query}, the validator,
played no part; none of the 50 had a rejection. On \mLuna{} the tools help
by putting the right clause in front of the agent as it writes. On
\mQwen{} they add 10.1 points on the rule-matching tasks (64 tasks to 16)
and trail the text on the rest (30 to 49).

\subsection{Complete prose carries even a procedure}
\label{sec:procedures}

DABStep's contract gain sits on its rule-matching tasks. Two blind raters
scored the procedural load of the knowledge each question needs, on both
benchmarks shuffled together. On DABStep the gain sits on the high-load
tasks (119 tasks to 20, none on the rest), and within them on the top
level (116 to 14), where every task is the fee procedure.
That left ``a rule-matching procedure'' and ``this procedure'' confounded,
so experiment 4 built new ones. On three LiveSQLBench databases from three
domains, each received a rule-matching procedure (400--520 seeded rules
matched on several fields, with wildcards, a monthly band, precedence and
a default) and a composite of dependent formulas, both stated completely,
in a clean version and in a version written like a business manual at
twice the length. Ten questions per piece and database, 60 in all. Every
knowledge arm, prose or contract, in either style, scores between 96.7
and 100\% on \mLuna{} and \mSol{}, and so does \mQwen{}, added after that
ceiling was seen. \textbf{Neither the type of knowledge nor the style of
the prose decides it}; on LiveSQLBench's own determined tasks the
high-load ones gain nothing from the contract either (8 tasks to 5,
$p{=}0.58$).

\paragraph{The preprint's account, revised.} We attributed the contract's
lead over the manual to delivery: the rule one lookup away rather than
buried in a long prompt. That explanation does not hold. Most of the lead
was two facts the manual did not state. With the facts worded alike, the
contract's form adds 4 to 6 points on the tasks its procedure governs, of
which part may be repetition. The governed tools add retrieval at the
point of use on some families and on some models. And
Section~\ref{sec:scale} tests burial directly: with up to four times the
base contract in the prompt, nothing is lost.
