\section{Beyond Accuracy}
\label{sec:buys}

With the facts equal, delivery moves accuracy by a few points
(Section~\ref{sec:delivery}). What else is the governed contract
for? Two things that delivery buys, each measured: it keeps cost nearly
flat as the contract grows, and it records which knowledge each answer
used. A third, safety, goes with stating the rules, however they are
delivered.

The gaps experiment found that looking knowledge up as needed saves input
but not money: one database's contract, about 25k tokens, fits in a cached
prompt, so loading it whole is nearly free. The scale experiment grows the
contract until that should change. The constructed questions' three
databases and 60 questions are kept, each with its own contract, and
padded with real but irrelevant contracts: the other two constructed
databases' first, then 19 contracts the author wrote for other
LiveSQLBench databases, each namespaced under its database's name, with
the original at the middle. Five levels take whole contracts, up to all 22
databases (Figure~\ref{fig:scale}). The largest level that both arms fit
on every model is 4x: \mQwen{}'s window does not fit the prompt arm beyond
it.

\textbf{Size costs no accuracy, up to 4x, in an easy regime.} The prompt
arm at 4x is below itself at 1x on 3 tasks and above on none: 3 of 540
runs lost, all on \mLuna{}, inside the registered $\pm$5-point
equivalence bound (37). Treating the runs as independent puts the loss
below 1.6\%; the runs share 60 questions in six pieces, so that bound
describes these questions, not a population of them. \mLuna{}'s prompt arm, with
all 22 databases (551k tokens) in its prompt, still scores 98.3\%, though
that level was not part of a registered test. With no drop to hold, the
predictions that the lookup arm's drop is smaller, or that it drops at
all, both fail. \mQwen{}'s lookup arm scores 100\% at 8x and at all 22
databases, where the prompt arm does not fit its window. The regime is the
easy one for long contexts: each question names the metric it needs word
for word, the padding is other databases under distinct names, the
questions sit at the ceiling at 1x, and the registered test stops at about
92k tokens. Long-context studies find the largest losses where the
question shares no words with what it needs, where distractors resemble
it, or where the task is harder~\cite{nolima,context-rot,ruler}, and some
loss from length alone~\cite{context-rot,length-alone-hurts}. At these lengths and with these questions
we saw none. The result says that a contract's size alone need not cost
accuracy; it is not a test of context rot.

\textbf{Cost is where the deliveries differ.} At one database the prompt
arm is as cheap or cheaper: its prompt is cached, and it takes 2.7 to 3.8
turns against the lookup arm's 5. From 2x the lookup arm is cheaper on the GPT models, on 58
of 60 tasks at 4x on \mLuna{} and on all 60 on \mSol{} (39), $p{<}10^{-10}$
on each: 1.7 and 2.4 times cheaper there, and 6.4 times cheaper on
\mLuna{} at 22 databases. The lookup arm's input tokens double from 1x to
22x, as its index grows; the prompt arm's grow 21-fold. On \mQwen{}, priced by its output alone, the prompt arm stays
cheaper at 4x. Under one fixed price the result holds fully cached and
uncached, and uncached the lookup
arm is cheaper even at one database. The traces show why the lookup arm's
cost stays flat: its lookups never name a padding database or metric, and
no query touches a padding table. Padding reaches its context only through
an unfiltered metric listing, in 0 to 1.7\% of runs on the GPT models and
up to 9\% on \mQwen{}. On the DABStep panels, per correct answer, the contract cost \mSol{} more
than the manual (59 against 47 thousandths of a dollar) and \mQwen{} less
(1.7 against 5.4)\ifextended; the median question took 28 seconds against
23 on \mSol{} and 37 against 45 on \mQwen{}\fi. Every row records its token counts,
so the cost can be recomputed at any price.

\subsection{A record of what each answer used}
\label{sec:audit}

Every governed lookup names the definition it returns, so the trace
records which knowledge each answer read before its query was written; a
log of the SQL alone shows only what was computed. This analysis was not
predicted. In the scale experiment the final SQL
contains a looked-up metric's expression verbatim in 91\% of \mLuna{}'s
lookup runs, 98\% of \mSol{}'s and 87\% of \mQwen{}'s runs with a lookup.
A departure does not mark a wrong answer: there every run that departed
from its looked-up formula was right. What the record does is locate an
error's source. Of the gaps experiment's 175 wrong governed answers, 174
are in the database whose contract carries the author's misses, and 90
applied the wrong definition verbatim; each names the contract entry that
produced it, to be fixed once for every later answer. The record does not
detect a wrong answer; it says where to fix one.

\subsection{Runs that state the rules do not write}
\label{sec:governance}

The contract declares forbidden operations, rendered into the prompt and
enforced by the tools' static checks. In the four single runs, where every
submitted statement was read, the arms without the contract's rules
submitted 166 mutating statements over 26 tasks and the governed arms
none. Every DABStep run since was checked by the harness's integrity test
on its own working copy of the database. On the seven models it found
\textbf{no corrupted copy in 29{,}356 runs whose prompt stated the
forbidden operations, against 50 in 21{,}790 whose prompt did not}
(0.23\%). The first group includes 7{,}218 runs of the contract's text over
the plain tools, where nothing enforced the rules. 44 of the 50 are
\mQwen{}'s, 42 of them in \armM{} and \armMw{}: 44 of its 8{,}421 runs
without the rules, none of its 8{,}421 with them; on the other models the
counts are too small to compare. One
escalation in the single runs shows the hazard: an agent lost a temporary
table to the plain tool's per-call connection, diagnosed it, and switched
to persistent tables (\appref{app:escalations}). The validator never had
to block a write, so this is not enforcement. Nor is it a clean test of
stating the rules: the arms that state them also hold the contract's
knowledge and workflow, which may leave less reason for a scratch table,
so it is an association that a rules-only arm would settle; and the later runs bound
writes that succeeded, not attempts. The plain tools here open a writable
connection, so the hazard is real, but a read-only role removes it
outright and remains the baseline; the stated rules matter where a
deployment cannot or does not grant one, and enforcement was never
exercised.
