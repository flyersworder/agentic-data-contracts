\section{Beyond Accuracy}
\label{sec:buys}

Section~\ref{sec:delivery} found no consistent accuracy difference between
the two routes once the facts are stated alike. Where accuracy is
comparable, the choice of route turns on other things. Two are measured
here: cost and capacity as the contract grows, and a log of what each
answer consulted. A third, the absence of writes, goes with stating the
rules, however they are delivered.

The gaps experiment found that looking knowledge up as needed saves input
but not money: one database's contract, about 25k tokens, fits in a cached
prompt, so loading it whole is nearly free. The scale experiment grows the
contract until that should change. The constructed questions' three
databases and 60 questions are kept, each with its own contract, and
padded with real but irrelevant contracts: the other two constructed
databases' first, then 19 contracts the author wrote for other
LiveSQLBench databases, each namespaced under its database's name, with
the original at the middle. Five levels take whole contracts, up to all 22
databases (Table~\ref{tab:scale}). The largest level that the lookup and YAML arms fit
on every model is 4x: \mQwen{}'s window does not fit the YAML arm beyond
it.

\textbf{Pasting the contract's YAML at 4x, about 92k tokens, met the
registered equivalence margin, in an easy regime.} The YAML arm at 4x lost
3 of 540 runs against itself at 1x, all on \mLuna{}, inside the registered
$\pm$5-point bound (37); at most 1.6\% for these 60 questions, which share
six pieces of knowledge. \mLuna{}'s YAML arm with all 22 databases (551k
tokens) still scores 98.3\% (unregistered), and \mQwen{}'s lookup arm 100\%
at 22 databases, where its YAML arm does not fit. With no drop to hold, the
predictions that the lookup arm's drop is smaller, or that it drops at
all, both fail. The regime is the easy
one for long contexts: each question names its metric, the padding is
other databases under distinct names, and the questions sit at the ceiling.
Long-context studies find the largest losses where the question shares no
words with what it needs, where distractors resemble it, or where the task
is harder~\cite{nolima,context-rot,ruler}, and some from length
alone~\cite{context-rot,length-alone-hurts}. The result says that a
contract's size alone need not cost accuracy here; it is not a test of
context rot.

\textbf{Cost and capacity are where the deliveries differ}
(Table~\ref{tab:scale}). At one database the YAML arm is as cheap or
cheaper: its prompt is cached, and it takes 2.7 to 3.8 turns against
lookup's 5. From 2x lookup is cheaper on the GPT models, on 58 of 60 tasks
at 4x on \mLuna{} and all 60 on \mSol{} (39), $p{<}10^{-10}$ on each, and
6.4 times cheaper on \mLuna{} at 22 databases; under one fixed price this
holds cached and uncached. The YAML is a verbose comparator. The same
knowledge as a prose KB costs about 1.1 times lookup's at 4x on the GPT
models and less at 1x, and it fits \mQwen{}'s window even at 22 databases
(about 99k tokens). Experiment~5e ran it there, co-run with the governed
contract. \textbf{The prose KB lost no accuracy at 22 databases}: 100\%
against 97.2\% at 1x, no run of 180 lost, as registered in (52) (exact
upper bound 2.0\%). It paid in input, 920k tokens a run against 133k for
lookup, which used fewer on all 60 questions, as registered in (53)
($p{=}9{\times}10^{-19}$): \$1.91 a run against \$0.29 at one fixed
reference price (\$2 per million input tokens). Lookup's cost stays flat
because its lookups never name a padding database or metric; padding
reaches its context only through an unfiltered metric listing, in 0 to
1.7\% of runs on the GPT models and up to 9\% on \mQwen{}. Every row
records its token counts, so the cost can be recomputed at any price.
\ifextended On the DABStep panels, per correct answer, the contract cost

\subsection{A log of what each answer consulted}
\label{sec:audit}

Every governed lookup names the definition it returns, so the trace is a
log of the definitions consulted before a query was written; a log of the
SQL alone shows only what was computed. The log is not provenance in the
database sense~\cite{buneman-provenance}: it records what was read, not
what the answer was derived from. A syntactic check adds part of that.
This analysis was not predicted. In the scale experiment the final SQL
contains a looked-up metric's expression verbatim in 91\% of \mLuna{}'s
lookup runs, 98\% of \mSol{}'s and 87\% of \mQwen{}'s runs with a lookup.
A departure does not mark a wrong answer: there every run that departed
from its looked-up formula was right. What the record does is locate an
error's source. Of the gaps experiment's 175 wrong governed answers, 174
are in the database whose contract carries the author's misses, and 90
applied the wrong definition verbatim; each names the contract entry that
produced it, to be fixed once for every later answer. The record does not
detect a wrong answer; it says where to fix one.

\subsection{No observed write where the rules were stated}
\label{sec:governance}

The contract declares forbidden operations, rendered into the prompt and
enforced by the tools' static checks. In the four single runs, where every
submitted statement was read, the arms without the contract's rules
submitted 166 mutating statements over 26 tasks and the governed arms
none. Every DABStep run since was checked by the harness's integrity test
on its own working copy of the database: \textbf{no corrupted copy in
36{,}574 runs whose prompt stated the forbidden operations, against 50 in
21{,}790 whose prompt did not}. Only \mQwen{} has events enough to compare:
44 of its 8{,}421 runs without the rules, none of its 12{,}030 with them,
and none of its 4{,}812 runs of the contract's text, which stated the rules
over the plain tools with nothing enforcing them ($p{=}3{\times}10^{-9}$,
expl.);
the other six models had 0 to 3 events each. The harness provokes the
hazard: the plain tool opens a writable connection per call, which drops
temporary tables, and agents that keep scratch results in them turned to
persistent tables (\appref{app:escalations}). The validator never had to
block a write, so this is not enforcement, and the arms that state the
rules also hold the contract's knowledge and workflow, so it is an
association that a rules-only arm would settle. A read-only role removes
the hazard outright and remains the baseline.
