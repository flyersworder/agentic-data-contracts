\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{The effect is domain-specific, and the benchmark cannot show
otherwise}

This is the sharpest limit on the result, so we give it first.

DABStep's hard set is dominated by one question family: 294 of 332 hard tasks
in run~A concern fees, and the contract encodes fee-domain semantics.

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Run & Bucket & \armS{} & \armH{} & \armM{} & \armC{} \\
\midrule
A & fee (294)     & 13.9\% & 18.7\% & 24.5\% & \textbf{59.2\%} \\
A & non-fee (38)  & 5 & 9 & 4 & 9 \\
B & fee (196)     & 23.0\% & 22.4\% & 44.9\% & \textbf{61.2\%} \\
B & non-fee (30)  & 6 & 7 & 9 & 8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Hard-task accuracy split by whether the question concerns the domain
the contract describes. Non-fee cells are counts, not rates.}
\label{tab:feebucket}
\end{table*}

``Hard accuracy'' on this benchmark is close to ``fee-question accuracy''. The
honest statement of the result is therefore: \textbf{a contract carrying a
domain's semantics produces a large gain on questions in that domain.}

\textbf{On the non-fee bucket there is no contract advantage on either model}
--- 9 against 4 in run~A, 8 against 9 in run~B, against \armM{}. At 38 and 30
tasks these are far too small to constitute a negative result, and they are
equally unable to support a general claim. Both are consistent with the effect
being domain-specific by construction: outside the domain the contract
describes, it is inert. That is the expected behaviour of a domain contract,
and it is also why this benchmark cannot answer the general question.

A 30-task sub-bucket (\emph{most expensive MCC for a transaction of $N$ euros})
scores 1--2 out of 30 for \emph{every} arm on \emph{both} models. A template no
arm solves is a property of the benchmark, and it bears on
Section~\ref{sec:interaction}: part of what looks like a ceiling may be this.

\subsection{One benchmark, 26 templates}

Cannot be fixed with compute. 450 tasks generated from 26 templates is a narrow
question space, and results on it should not be read as results about analytics
agents in general. We report it plainly rather than hedging around it.

\subsection{$k{=}1$}

Neither run repeats. One task was observed flipping verdict between two
identical runs during development, so per-task verdicts are not stable. With
86--155 discordant pairs the headline comparisons are not at risk, but
\textbf{nothing in this paper estimates within-condition variance}: our two
runs are different models, not replicates. This is the largest remaining gap.

\subsection{Reconstructed golds}

401 of 450 tasks, by plurality over officially-graded-correct submissions at a
0.75 threshold, with five verified-wrong golds excluded and one template family
(59/59) independently reproduced from the database. See
Section~\ref{sec:golds}. The reader cannot check this independently, which is
why we plan the external validation described there.

\subsection{Run B's truncation}

29\% of rows lost to provider rate-limiting, near-uniform across arms, with
114 of 122 lost tasks losing all four arms together. Detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:truncation}. It costs power, not validity, and it widens
run~B's intervals by roughly 20\% relative to run~A.

We draw one methodological lesson from it that generalises beyond this paper.
Pinning a single provider endpoint with fallbacks disabled is correct for
validity --- it is what holds quantization, price and throughput fixed across
arms --- but it transfers responsibility for transient failures to the harness.
Ours had no retry for HTTP 429, so a temporary condition became 466 permanent
rows. The sweep exited cleanly and finished ahead of schedule, and nothing
looked wrong until the results were read. Benchmark harnesses that pin
endpoints need a rate-of-progress check, not just a liveness check.

\subsection{The two runs are not perfectly comparable}

Run~A's governed arms carried a validator defect: a check intended to reject
\texttt{SELECT *} also rejected \texttt{COUNT(*)}, because the latter parses
with a \texttt{Star} node. The agent was told \emph{``SELECT * is not
allowed''} about queries containing no \texttt{SELECT *} --- 161 rejections
across 124 of 401 tasks, of which replay showed 79 of 160 distinct queries were
falsely rejected.

A rejection whose stated reason is not true of the query cannot be complied
with, and the transcripts show the cost: on task 1278 the model reissued the
identical query, then guessed at unrelated clauses (\emph{``maybe it flags
AVG(''}) before finding a formulation that passed.

This handicapped both contract arms and neither baseline, so run~A's 55.1\% is
a floor rather than a ceiling. Run~B ran with the fix and its inspect-rejection
count falls from 218 to 29, confirming the diagnosis. The defect's direction is
known; its magnitude is not separable from the model change, and we do not
attempt to estimate it.

The runs also used different provider endpoints. Each run's internal contrasts
are sound; cross-run differences are suggestive only.

\subsection{Self-attested provenance}

The contract's header declares that every fact in it came from
\texttt{manual.md} and the dataset README. A reader can verify that it contains
nothing beyond those documents. Nobody outside the project can verify that no
benchmark question was consulted while it was authored. We state this rather
than argue it away.

\subsection{Harness failures are reported, not hidden}

Not every non-correct row is a wrong answer: an arm can hit an output cap or
raise. Excluding such rows flatters whichever arm fails most; counting them as
wrong punishes an arm for a harness defect. We report both views for every arm
throughout and flag any arm whose harness-failure rate crosses a threshold.

One design decision here is worth surfacing. When an agent exhausts its output
budget, the harness forces a final answer rather than discarding the run. In
run~A this produced only one additional correct answer out of 59 --- nearly
worthless for accuracy --- but it eliminated unscoreable rows, and those rows
were 41 \armS{}, 18 \armM{} and \textbf{zero} in either governed arm. Dropping
them would have inflated the two baselines by silently discarding their hardest
runs. Run~B repeats the pattern: 55, 42, 15 and zero.
