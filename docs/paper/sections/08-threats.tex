\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{The effect is domain-specific, and the benchmark cannot show
otherwise}

This is the sharpest limit on the result, so we give it first.

DABStep's hard set is dominated by one question family: 294 of 332 hard tasks
in run~A concern fees, and the contract encodes fee-domain semantics
(Table~\ref{tab:feebucket}).

``Hard accuracy'' on this benchmark is close to ``fee-question accuracy'', so
the result should be stated as: \textbf{a contract carrying a domain's semantics produces
a large gain on questions in that domain.}

\textbf{On the non-fee bucket there is no detectable contract advantage on any
model} ---
against \armM{}, 9 to 4 in run~A, 8 to 9 in run~B, 11 to 10 in run~C, 11 to 8
in run~D. At 38, 30, 38 and 38 tasks these are far too small to be a negative
result, and equally unable to support a general claim. On run~C the ordering inverts outright:
\armS{} leads that slice at 14 of 38 while \armC{} takes 11 --- the only
top-level slice anywhere in the four runs where the bare baseline beats the
contract. All
four are consistent with the effect being domain-specific by construction:
outside the domain the contract describes it is inert, and a frontier-class model
does not rescue it. That is the expected behaviour of a domain contract, and it
is why this benchmark cannot answer the general question.

A 30-task sub-bucket inside the fee bucket (\emph{most expensive MCC}, or
\emph{ACI}, \emph{for a transaction of $N$ euros}; 10 and 20 tasks) scores 0--3 of 30 for
\emph{every} arm on runs~A, B and~D. Run~C partially solves it (\armS{} 8,
\armH{} 5, \armC{} 4, \armM{} 2 of 30), which is a second slice where the bare
baseline leads.

The benchmark is also a single one, and a concentrated one: 450 tasks reduce
to 113 question shapes, 27 of which cover three quarters of the benchmark
(Section~\ref{sec:background}). No amount of compute fixes that, and results
on so narrow a question space should not be read as results about analytics
agents in general.

\subsection{The contract resolves ambiguities the manual leaves open}
\label{sec:threats-interpretation}

The arms are matched on the sources they may consult, not on how those sources
read. \texttt{semantic.yml} marks seven clauses with a sentence beginning
\texttt{INTERPRETATION}, flagging where a metric commits to a reading the
prose does not force. Three are glosses of wording the manual supplies
outright. One derives the \texttt{capture\_delay} day-count-to-band mapping by
applying the manual's own band definitions to a column of days. The remaining
three go further, and we state them plainly: the manual says a \texttt{null}
field ``applies to all possible values'', but the annexed \texttt{fees} data
encodes that wildcard for list-typed fields as an \emph{empty list}, which the
manual never mentions; and two band metrics break a tie on values sitting
exactly on a shared boundary, which the manual's ``between A and B'' does not
decide.

All three are recoverable from the annexed data, which every arm could query,
and none supplies a task's answer. But they were recovered once, by us, and
handed to one arm. The empty-list clause in particular is load-bearing:
reversing it in the compiled contract changes the answer on 87 of the 97
tasks in the payments families, so an agent lacking the clause has somewhere
to go wrong. A reader should
therefore treat the contract arm's margin as combining two things this
experiment does not separate --- delivery of what the manual states, and
resolution of what it leaves open. Separating them needs a fifth arm carrying
the contract with its \texttt{INTERPRETATION} clauses stripped, which we did
not run.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Statistical power: $k{=}1$, and 24
uncorrected tests}{Statistical power: k=1, and 24 uncorrected tests}}

None of the four runs repeats, so none of them estimates within-condition
variance: they are different models, not replicates. One near-replicate
exists. A single-arm sweep of \armC{} over all 450 tasks, run on
\texttt{glm-5.3-flash} for a leaderboard submission, shares 401 tasks with
run~A's \armC{} and differs from it only by a validator fix that run~A
predates. Paired, the two agree on accuracy --- 60.3\% against 57.9\%,
exact McNemar $p{=}0.35$ --- and \textbf{disagree on 94 of 401 tasks, a
23.4\% flip rate}, at temperature 0 on a pinned endpoint. On the hard split
it is 25.9\%; on the easy split both runs score exactly 49/69 while
disagreeing about which 8 tasks. Because the two runs also differ by that
validator fix, 23.4\% is an upper bound on run-to-run noise rather than a
clean estimate of it.

Two consequences. The headline comparisons are not at risk: McNemar reads
asymmetry, and this churn is symmetric (52 one way, 42 the other). But our
contrasts rest on 86--182 discordant pairs and noise alone supplies 94, so a
contrast near that floor is close to the noise, and \textbf{no claim about a
named task's verdict should be read as reproducible}. Repeats remain the
largest gap for runs~B, C and~D, and largest for the last two:
\texttt{gpt-5.6-sol} does not accept a temperature parameter, and run~D's
gateway rejects both \texttt{temperature} and \texttt{seed}, so neither ran
at 0 like the first two --- and the glm figure suggests their flip rates are
higher, not lower.

The pre-registration also named a primary comparison, on
\texttt{deepseek-v4-pro-0813}, that was never run: a 40-task probe showed it
would not supply a third capability point, and runs~C and~D were chosen in its
place after that probe. Every contrast involving them is therefore
exploratory, the derivation-gap result included, and the public prediction of
Section~\ref{sec:derivation} was recorded against run~C after the substitution
was made.

Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} reports 24 pairwise comparisons and we correct none of
them. The three vs-\armC{} contrasts survive any correction at these
$p$-values; the \armS{}-vs-\armH{} row, which carries the ablation, does not
have that margin on runs~A and~B and should be read with Benjamini--Hochberg in
mind. Underpowered comparisons are the norm in this literature rather than the
exception~\cite{little-power}.

\subsection{Contamination}

DABStep is public, and so is the material an agent needs to answer it. Our own
gold reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:golds}) makes the point sharply: we
recovered 59 hard-split answers from public sources alone, which is a
demonstration that the answer key exists in machine-readable form outside the
withheld split. Nothing stops that material from having entered a model's
training data, and we ran no contamination probe --- no canary strings, no
perturbed restatements of the questions, no comparison against a
post-cutoff held-out set.

This bears asymmetrically on the capability claims. Our four models differ
in training data as well as in capability, and nothing here separates the
two, so ``stronger model'' and ``different training data'' are confounded
across every comparison in Section~\ref{sec:derivation}. A memorising model would
show exactly what we report: high accuracy on the \emph{macro} bucket, whose
tasks follow the benchmark's most common shapes, and a smaller gain on the
\emph{derived} bucket, whose tasks compose them. We do not believe this
explains the result, because \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}'s contract arm scores
94.9\% against 31.8\% for its schema-only arm on the same tasks, and Sonnet~5's
73.9\% against 6.8\%, and memorisation
should not care which context the agent was handed. But that argument is an
inference, not a measurement, and a contamination probe would settle what it
only makes plausible.

\subsection{Two frontier-class points that disagree on size, and a trend that
already broke once}

The capability claims in this paper rest on two frontier-class models, and we
have direct evidence for how little one supported: two claims written against
runs~A and~B did not survive run~C (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation}
and~\ref{sec:interaction}), and a third,
written against runs~A--C, did not survive run~D. \textbf{Readers should apply
the same discount to the derivation-gap result} (Section~\ref{sec:derivation}):
the second frontier-class model confirms the direction of the collapse (26.1
points, between the flash-tier models' ${\approx}38$ and run~C's 5.1) and not
its size. The two frontier-class models disagree by 21 points on the
\emph{macro} bucket and by 4, in the other direction, on \emph{derived}.
Capability is also not one number
here. Sonnet~5 ties \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} on the bare-schema arm we use
as the capability axis and sits 12 points above it with the contract; the two
orderings agree on every model's rank but not on the spacing, and every
``ordered by capability'' statement in this paper depends on which arm is
doing the ordering. The literature does not settle the direction either. Some
prompt scaffolding does lose value as models
improve~\cite{prompt-engineering-advanced}; but a pre-registered study across
five frontier models found the opposite on GAIA, with the most capable model
gaining \emph{most} from structured scaffolds~\cite{scaffold-gaia}. Runs~C
and~D also differ from the others in provider and model family, so any
contrast between the frontier-class runs and the flash-tier ones confounds
capability with both.

\subsection{Harness asymmetries}
\label{sec:harness-asym}

Three properties of the harness treated the arms unequally, two against the
governed arms and one, small, in their favour; a fourth makes the runs less
comparable to each other.

\paragraph{A validator defect, run~A only.} Run~A's governed arms carried a
defect in the validator: a check intended to reject \texttt{SELECT *} also
rejected \texttt{COUNT(*)}, because the latter parses with a \texttt{Star}
node. The agent was told \emph{``SELECT * is not allowed''} about queries
containing no \texttt{SELECT *} --- 161 rejections across 124 of 401 tasks;
replaying the 160 distinct rejected queries showed 79 were falsely rejected. A rejection whose stated reason is not true of the query cannot be
complied with, and the transcripts show the agent guessing at unrelated clauses
before finding a formulation that passed.

This handicapped both contract arms and neither baseline, so run~A's 55.1\% is a
floor rather than a ceiling. Run~B ran with the fix, and the contract arm's
total count of queries rejected by the validator (218 on run~A, the 161
above among them) falls to 29, confirming the diagnosis. The defect's direction is known; its magnitude
is not separable from the model change, and we do not attempt to estimate it.

\paragraph{Different endpoints across runs.} The runs used different provider
endpoints as well as different models, and run~D a different kind of route
altogether: an enterprise gateway fronting Bedrock, on the Messages API with
cache control. Run~D's dollar figures are that gateway's billed rates under
Messages-API cache pricing; on the same gateway's OpenAI-compatible route the
arms would have been billed at multiples that differ by arm
(Section~\ref{sec:runs}), so the run~D cost ordering is a property of the
route as well as of the arms. Each run's internal contrasts are sound;
cross-run differences are suggestive only.

\paragraph{The row limit.} Symmetric result limits are not a detail. An
earlier iteration of this experiment used a 1{,}000-row limit, and the
governed arm looked $11{\times}$ more expensive than the baselines; the cause
was oversized result payloads poisoning the context window, not the contract.
The reported runs cap every result at 50 rows, but not perfectly
symmetrically: the cap reached the governed arms' \texttt{run\_query} but not
their \texttt{preview\_table}, which the library clamps at 100 rows of its own
accord. A governed arm could therefore see 100 rows from a preview where an
ungoverned arm saw 50 from \texttt{execute\_sql}. That happened on
\textbf{31 preview calls} across runs~A--C, against many thousands of tool
calls: small, but in the treatment's favour. Run~D ran with the cap in place
(484 preview calls, none over 50 rows); runs~A--C are as they ran.

\paragraph{The forced-answer policy.} When an agent exhausts its output budget the harness forces a final answer
instead of discarding the run. In run~A that produced one additional correct
answer out of 59, nearly worthless for accuracy, but it eliminated unscoreable
rows --- and those rows were 41 \armS{}, 18 \armM{} and \textbf{zero} in either
governed arm. Dropping them would have inflated the two baselines by silently
discarding their hardest runs. Run~B repeats the pattern (49, 38, 15 and zero
on the complete-case set; 55, 42 and 15 before it), run~C forces 8 answers in
\armH{} and none elsewhere, and run~D forces 43, 32, 27 and zero. \armC{} is
the only arm that never exhausted its budget on any run. The policy therefore works against the treatment, and we
report scored and strict accuracy for every arm throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
