\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{The effect is domain-specific, and the benchmark cannot show
otherwise}

This is the sharpest limit on the result, so we give it first.

DABStep's hard set is dominated by one question family: 294 of 332 hard tasks
in run~A concern fees, and the contract encodes fee-domain semantics
(Table~\ref{tab:feebucket}).

``Hard accuracy'' on this benchmark is close to ``fee-question accuracy'', so
the result states as: \textbf{a contract carrying a domain's semantics produces
a large gain on questions in that domain.}

\textbf{On the non-fee bucket there is no contract advantage on any model} ---
against \armM{}, 9 to 4 in run~A, 8 to 9 in run~B, 11 to 10 in run~C. At 38, 30
and 38 tasks these are far too small to be a negative result, and equally
unable to support a general claim. On run~C the ordering inverts outright:
\armS{} leads that slice at 14 of 38 while \armC{} takes 11 --- the only slice
anywhere in the three runs where the bare baseline beats the contract. All
three are consistent with the effect being domain-specific by construction:
outside the domain the contract describes it is inert, and a frontier model
does not rescue it. That is the expected behaviour of a domain contract, and it
is why this benchmark cannot answer the general question.

A 30-task sub-bucket (\emph{most expensive MCC for a transaction of $N$ euros})
scores 1--2 out of 30 for \emph{every} arm on runs~A and~B. Run~C partially
solves it (\armS{} 8, \armH{} 5, \armC{} 4, \armM{} 2 of 30), which is a second
slice where the bare baseline leads.

\subsection{The contract resolves ambiguities the manual leaves open}
\label{sec:threats-interpretation}

The arms are matched on the sources they may consult, not on how those sources
read. \texttt{semantic.yml} marks seven clauses with a sentence beginning
\texttt{INTERPRETATION}, flagging where a metric commits to a reading the
prose does not force. Three are glosses of wording the manual supplies
outright. One derives the \texttt{capture\_delay} day-count-to-band mapping by
applying the manual's own band definitions to a column of days. The remaining
three go further, and we state them plainly: the manual says a \texttt{null}
field ``applies to all possible values,'' but the annexed \texttt{fees} data
encodes that wildcard for list-typed fields as an \emph{empty list}, which the
manual never mentions; and two band metrics break a tie on values sitting
exactly on a shared boundary, which the manual's ``between A and B'' does not
decide.

All three are recoverable from the annexed data, which every arm could query,
and none supplies a task's answer. But they were recovered once, by us, and
handed to one arm. The empty-list clause in particular is load-bearing: the
counterfactual lesion that reverses it is diagnostic on 87 of the 97 tasks in
the payments families: on those tasks, a macro built with the misreading
returns a different answer from the one built with the clause, so an agent
lacking the clause has somewhere to go wrong. A reader should
therefore treat the contract arm's margin as combining two things this
experiment does not separate --- delivery of what the manual states, and
resolution of what it leaves open. Separating them needs a fifth arm carrying
the contract with its \texttt{INTERPRETATION} clauses stripped, which we did
not run.

\subsection{One benchmark, and a concentrated one}

No amount of compute fixes this. 450 tasks reduce to 113 question shapes, 27
of which cover three quarters of the benchmark. That is a narrow question
space, and results on it should not be read as results about analytics agents
in general.

\subsection{$k{=}1$}

No run repeats. One task was observed flipping verdict between two identical
runs during development, so per-task verdicts are not stable. With 86--164
discordant pairs the headline comparisons are not at risk, but \textbf{nothing
in this paper estimates within-condition variance}: our three runs are
different models, not replicates. This is the largest remaining gap, and run~C
needs it most, because \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol} does not accept a temperature
parameter and therefore ran at the provider's default rather than at 0 like the
other two.

\subsection{Eighteen tests, no multiplicity correction}

Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} reports 18 pairwise comparisons and we correct none of
them. The three vs-\armC{} contrasts survive any correction at these
$p$-values; the \armS{}-vs-\armH{} row, which carries the ablation, does not
have that margin on runs~A and~B and should be read with Benjamini--Hochberg in
mind. Under-powered comparisons are the norm in this literature rather than the
exception~\cite{little-power}.

\subsection{Contamination}

DABStep is public, and so is the material an agent needs to answer it. Our own
gold reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:golds}) makes the point sharply: we
recovered 59 hard-split answers from public sources alone, which is a
demonstration that the answer key exists in machine-readable form outside the
withheld split. Nothing stops that material from having entered a model's
training data, and we ran no contamination probe --- no canary strings, no
perturbed restatements of the questions, no comparison against a
post-cutoff-held-out set.

This bears asymmetrically on the capability claims. Our three models are
ordered by capability, but they are also ordered by release date, so
``stronger model'' and ``more recent training data'' are collinear across
every comparison in Section~\ref{sec:derivation}. A memorising model would
show exactly what we report: high accuracy on the \emph{macro} bucket, whose
tasks follow the benchmark's most common shapes, and a smaller gain on the
\emph{derived} bucket, whose tasks compose them. We do not believe this
explains the result, because the frontier model's contract arm scores 94.9\%
while its schema-only arm scores 31.8\% on the same tasks, and memorisation
should not care which context the agent was handed. But that argument is an
inference, not a measurement, and a contamination probe would settle what it
only makes plausible.

\subsection{One frontier point, and a trend that already broke once}

Every capability claim in this paper rests on a single strong model. We have
direct evidence for how little that supports: with two models the contract's
margin over a hand-written prompt fell from $+32.2$ to $+13.7$, we reported the
shrinking margin as a finding, and the third model returned $+27.1$. The
scaffolding null failed the same way. \textbf{Readers should apply the same
discount to the derivation-gap result} (Section~\ref{sec:derivation}), which is
a three-point curve with one frontier point, however sharp the collapse from
39.2 to 5.1 looks. The literature does not settle the direction either. Some
prompt scaffolding does lose value as models
improve~\cite{prompt-engineering-advanced}; but a pre-registered study across
five frontier models found the opposite on GAIA, with the most capable model
gaining \emph{most} from structured scaffolds~\cite{scaffold-gaia}. Run~C also differs from the others in provider and model
family, so any run-C contrast confounds capability with both.

\subsection{Three limits recorded elsewhere}

Golds cover 401 of 450 tasks, by plurality over officially-graded-correct
submissions at a 0.75 threshold, with five verified-wrong golds excluded and
one template family (59/59) reproduced from the database
(Section~\ref{sec:golds}); a reader cannot check this independently, which is
why we plan the external validation described there. Run~B lost 29\% of its
rows to provider rate-limiting, near-uniformly across arms, 114 of the 122 lost
tasks losing all four arms together (Section~\ref{sec:truncation}); it costs
power, not validity, and widens run~B's intervals by roughly 20\% relative to
run~A. And nobody outside the project can verify that no benchmark question was
consulted while the contract was authored (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

We draw one methodological lesson from it that generalises beyond this paper.
Pinning a single provider endpoint with fallbacks disabled is correct for
validity, since it holds quantization, price and throughput fixed across arms,
but it transfers responsibility for transient failures to the harness.
Ours had no retry for HTTP 429, so a temporary condition became 466 permanent
rows. The sweep exited cleanly and finished ahead of schedule, and nothing
looked wrong until the results were read. Benchmark harnesses that pin
endpoints need a rate-of-progress check, not just a liveness check.

\subsection{The runs are not perfectly comparable}

Run~A's governed arms carried a validator defect: a check intended to reject
\texttt{SELECT *} also rejected \texttt{COUNT(*)}, because the latter parses
with a \texttt{Star} node. The agent was told \emph{``SELECT * is not allowed''}
about queries containing no \texttt{SELECT *} --- 161 rejections across 124 of
401 tasks, of which replay showed 79 of 160 distinct queries were falsely
rejected. A rejection whose stated reason is not true of the query cannot be
complied with, and the transcripts show the agent guessing at unrelated clauses
before finding a formulation that passed.

This handicapped both contract arms and neither baseline, so run~A's 55.1\% is a
floor rather than a ceiling. Run~B ran with the fix and its inspect-rejection
count falls from 218 to 29, confirming the diagnosis. The defect's direction is
known; its magnitude is not separable from the model change, and we do not
attempt to estimate it. The runs also used different provider endpoints. Each
run's internal contrasts are sound; cross-run differences are suggestive only.

\subsection{The forced-answer policy}

When an agent exhausts its output budget the harness forces a final answer
instead of discarding the run. In run~A that produced one additional correct
answer out of 59, nearly worthless for accuracy, but it eliminated unscoreable
rows --- and those rows were 41 \armS{}, 18 \armM{} and \textbf{zero} in either
governed arm. Dropping them would have inflated the two baselines by silently
discarding their hardest runs. Run~B repeats the pattern (49, 38, 15 and zero
on the complete-case set; 55, 42 and 15 before it), and run~C forces 8 answers
in \armH{} and none elsewhere. \armC{} is the only arm that never exhausted its
budget on any run. The policy therefore works against the treatment, and we
report scored and strict accuracy for every arm throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
