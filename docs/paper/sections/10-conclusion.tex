\section{Conclusion}

Context layers work. The open question was which part of one does the work, and
it matters because the parts cost different amounts: semantic prose is written
once per domain and often already exists, retrieval scaffolding is engineering
paid once, and pre-computed macros are paid per metric forever.

Holding the tool surface, the retrieval instruction, the table allow-list and
the operation rules byte-for-byte fixed and emptying only the prose, the
treatment arm falls to the bare-schema baseline on both models. The scaffolding
term is not distinguishable from zero ($p{=}0.058$, $p{=}0.61$, pooled
$p{=}0.29$), and the two runs disagree on its sign. \textbf{The content does
the work.}

Delivery matters as much as possession: the same governing rule was present in
both the contract arm and the 24{,}177-character prompt arm, and only the
contract arm applied it. And the same runs show that declared operation rules
suppress mutation attempts entirely --- 144 in ungoverned arms, zero in
governed ones --- by deterrence rather than interception.

Three limits bound all of this. The gain appears only inside the domain the
contract describes, and the non-fee tasks show no advantage on either model.
The benchmark is a single one with 26 templates. And neither run repeats, so
nothing here estimates within-condition variance.

\paragraph{Future work.} Three items, in order of what a reader should want
most. Repeat runs, to put a variance estimate under every number here. A
stronger model, to separate the two readings of the capability interaction in
Section~\ref{sec:interaction} --- the pre-registered comparison for this study
names one, and reporting it is a debt this paper owes. And an executable metric
layer, built \emph{after} this measurement rather than before it, so that the
declarative and executable contributions can be told apart the way content and
scaffolding are told apart here.
